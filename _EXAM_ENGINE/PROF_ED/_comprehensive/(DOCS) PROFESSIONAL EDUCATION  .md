Historical Foundations Of Education
	•	Primitive Education – Survival and basic skills.
	•	Sumerians (Mesopotamia) – First civilization with formal schooling.
	•	Oriental Education – Focus on cultural preservation (e.g., Chinese, Egyptian).
	•	Greek Education:
	•	Athenian – Intellectual training.
	•	Spartan – Military and physical training.
	•	Roman Education – National success and citizenship.
	•	Early Christian Education – Religion-based formation.
	•	Germany (Froebel) – Origin of Kindergarten; emphasized learning through play.

Eastern And Middle Eastern Philosophy

1. Confucianism (Confucius / Kung Fu Tzu):
	•	Analects – Collection of teachings.
	•	Golden Rule – “Do not do to others what you do not want done to yourself.”
	•	Meritocracy – Governance based on merit.
	•	Humanistic values – Emphasis on family and social harmony.
	•	Life is a gift that must be treasured.
	•	Confucius – Advocated education for all, moral development, and social harmony.
	•	Mencius – Believed that humans are naturally good.
	•	Lao Tzu – Advocated natural flow, spontaneity, and the principle of Wu Wei (let things happen naturally).

2. Taoism (Lao Tzu):
	•	Yin and Yang – Balance in nature and life.
	•	Wu Wei – “Let things come naturally” / effortless action.
3. Hinduism – Influenced by teachings of Mahatma Gandhi.
	•	Vedas – Ancient sacred hymns and chants.
	•	Upanishads – Sacred philosophical texts discussing meditation, morality, and the nature of reality.
	•	Epics: Ramayana and Mahabharata – Teach values, divinity, and duties.
	•	Focus on divinity of the soul and control of desires.
	•	Hedonism – Belief that pleasure is the highest good.
	•	Stoicism – Belief that virtue is the path to happiness through self-control.

4. Buddhism:
	•	Founded by Siddhartha Gautama (Buddha).
	•	Goal: Freedom from suffering, achieve enlightenment and Nirvana.
	•	Emphasizes the Middle Way – balance and moderation.

5. Islamic Philosophy:
	•	Prophet Mohammad – Founder of Islam.
	•	Core teaching: Submission to Allah.
	•	Belief in Theism – one or more gods exist within the universe.
	•	Divine Attributes:
	•	Omniscient – All-knowing.
	•	Omnipotent – All-powerful.
	•	Omnipresent – Present everywhere.

THE PROFESSIONAL TEACHER
A profession is a vocation founded upon specialized training and a body of knowledge. Teachers are professionals because they:
	•	Undergo formal education
	•	Are licensed through a Licensure Examination for Teachers (LET)
	•	Adhere to a Code of Ethics
	•	Engage in Continuing Professional Development (CPD)
Professionalization Of Teaching
 P.D. 1006 – Decree Professionalizing Teachers
	•	Teachers must pass the PBET
	•	Minimum rating: 70%, no sub-score below 50%
	•	Penalties for practice without license: fine or imprisonment
 RA 7836 – Teachers Professionalization Act of 1994
	•	Created the Professional Board for Teachers (PRC)
	•	Prescribed the LET for teaching eligibility
	•	Board Composition: Chair + 4 members
	•	LET Required for:
	•	Preschool: BECED
	•	Elementary: BSEED
	•	Secondary: Bachelor’s with major/minor + 10 units Prof Ed
	•	Vocational: Bachelor’s + 18 units Prof Ed
Grounds for Revocation of License:
	•	Immorality
	•	Fraud
	•	Malpractice
	•	Drug abuse
	•	Code of Ethics violation
	•	Non-compliance with CPD

 RA 9293 – Amendment to RA 7836
	•	Para-teachers and inactive teachers (5 yrs) need refresher courses
	•	Special Permits for international practice

THE TEACHER AND THE SCHOOL CURRICULUM

Curriculum And The Teacher
Key Concepts:
	•	Curriculum is the heart of the teaching profession.
	•	Without curriculum: no education (formal, non-formal, or informal).
	•	Classrooms would be empty and teachers would have no direction.
	•	Teachers are always guided by a curriculum, whether written or not. It's the backbone of all school activities and learning processes.
The Sabre-Tooth Curriculum by Harold Benjamin (1939)
	•	A satirical story written in 1939 that reflects how outdated educational practices persist even when they are no longer useful.
	•	Originally referred to organized knowledge taught in the 19th century.
	•	Main point: Curriculum must evolve with time; otherwise, it becomes irrelevant.
	•	Curriculum has now broadened to include multiple forms of learning—formal, informal, experiential, and contextual.



Definitions Of Curriculum
Scholar
Definition
Daniel Tanner (1980)
Curriculum is a planned and guided set of learning experiences and intended outcomes, formulated through systematic reconstruction of knowledge and experiences under the auspices of the school.
Pratt (1980)
Curriculum is a written document that systematically describes goals, objectives, content, learning activities, evaluation procedures, and more.
Schubert (1987)
Curriculum includes the contents of a subject, concepts and tasks to be acquired, planned activities, desired outcomes, cultural products, and a reform agenda.
Hass (1987)
Curriculum includes all the experiences that learners have in a program of education planned to achieve broad goals and specific objectives.
Grundy (1987)
Curriculum is a program of activities (by teachers and pupils) designed so that learners will attain certain educational and other schooling goals.
Goodland & Su (1992)
Curriculum is a plan that provides learning opportunities within a set timeframe and environment, aiming for behavioral change in students through guided experiences.
Cronbeth (1992)
Curriculum answers the questions: What knowledge, skills, and values are most worthwhile? Why are they worthwhile? How should learners acquire them?

Curriculum Views

Traditional Points Of View
Scholar
View on Curriculum
Robert M. Hutchins
Curriculum should emphasize permanent studies—rules of grammar, reading, rhetoric, logic, and mathematics. Focus is on 3Rs in basic education and liberal education in college.
Arthur Bestor
An essentialist who believes curriculum should focus on intellectual training. Emphasizes subjects like grammar, literature, writing, mathematics, science, history, and foreign language.
Joseph Schwab
Curriculum should be based on academic disciplines such as Science, Mathematics, Social Studies, and English. He coined the term “discipline” as the source of curriculum.
Philip Phenix
Curriculum must consist of organized knowledge derived from various disciplines.

Scholar(s)

Defined curriculum as all experiences learners have under the guidance of teachers.

Curriculum is a sequence of potential experiences intended to develop group thinking and social behavior among learners.

Approaches To Curriculum
Approach
Meaning
1. Curriculum as Content
Focuses on what to teach (topics or subject matter).
Ways to present: topical, conceptual, thematic, modular.
Selection criteria: significance, validity, utility, learnability, feasibility, interest.
Guidelines: based on daily use, age level, career relevance, and subject connection.
Principles: balance, articulation , sequence, integration, continuity 
2. Curriculum as a Process
Focuses on how to teach (methods and strategies). Also known as PCK or pedagogical content knowledge.
3. Curriculum as a Product
Focuses on what learners achieve (knowledge, skills, values) after instruction.





Curriculum Development Models
Model
Description
Tyler Model
Focuses on 4 key questions:
1) What are the goals?
2) What experiences help reach them?
3) How should they be organized?
4) How do we know if goals are met?
Taba Model
A 7-step grassroots approach that starts with teachers and learner needs:
1) Diagnose needs
2) Set objectives
3) Select content
4) Organize content
5) Select learning experiences
6) Organize experiences
7) Evaluate outcomes
Saylor & Alexander Model
A 4-step model similar to the development process:
1) Set goals/objectives
2) Design the curriculum
3) Implement it
4) Evaluate and revise as needed

Peter Oliva
Promoted collaborative curriculum planning; emphasized that teachers and curriculum specialists should work as partners in developing and improving curriculum.

Types Of Classroom Management
1. Authoritative / Democratic
	•	Democratic teachers are kind but firm, promoting shared responsibility and clear expectations for behavior and performance.
	•	They encourage rather than demand, helping build self-esteem, even when students make mistakes.

2. Authoritarian
	•	Authoritarian teachers impose strict control, using power, pressure, and criticism to enforce rules.
	•	They make all decisions, often leading to student fear, low self-esteem, and reluctance to participate due to powerlessness.

3. Permissive / Laissez-Faire
	•	Places few demands or controls on students.
	•	Teacher accepts students’ impulses and actions, and is less likely to monitor their behavior.
	•	More concerned with the student’s emotional well-being than with classroom control.
	•	“Anything goes” environment usually leads to chaos.
	•	Classroom is often disorganized, causing student frustration, high stress, and a feeling of being overwhelmed and lost.



4. Uninvolved
	•	Teachers who are indifferent and undemanding of student involvement.
Approaches To Classroom Management
1. Assertive Approach
	•	Expects teachers to specify rules of behavior and consequences for disobeying them.
	•	Teachers must communicate these rules and consequences clearly.
	•	Teachers expect students to behave in a certain way in class (Duke and Mechel).

2. Behavior Modification Approach
	•	Strives to increase the occurrence of appropriate behavior through a system of rewards.
	•	Aims to reduce the likelihood of inappropriate behavior through punishments.

3. Behavioral Academic Approach
	•	Developed by Evertson and Emmer.
	•	Emphasizes the organization and management of students as they engage in academic work.
	•	Focuses on creating a smooth-running classroom where learning is maximized through effective routines, procedures, and transitions.

4. Group Managerial Approach
	•	Based on Jacob Kounin's research.
	•	Emphasizes the importance of responding immediately to group behavior that might be inappropriate or undesirable.
	•	Focuses on the teacher’s ability to manage the whole group and prevent misbehavior from escalating.
  a. Ripple Effect
	•	Occurs when a teacher corrects misbehavior in one student, and this positively influences others nearby.
	•	The correction serves as a warning or reminder for the rest.


 b. Withitness
	•	The teacher’s skill to know what is going on in all parts of the classroom at all times.
	•	Often described as having "eyes in the back of one's head."

 c. Pygmalion Effect / Rosenthal Effect
	•	The phenomenon where the greater the expectation placed upon people, the better they perform.
	•	Named after the Greek myth of Pygmalion, a sculptor whose statue came to life.



d. Hawthorne Effect (Observer Effect)
	•	A type of reactivity where individuals modify or improve behavior because they are aware of being observed.

e. John Henry Effect
	•	The opposite of the Hawthorne Effect.
	•	Occurs when a control group, aware of their status, puts in extra effort and achieves similar results to the experimental group.

f. Placebo Effect
	•	A phenomenon where a fake treatment (like sugar pills or saline solution) improves a condition simply because the person expects it to help.

g. Halo Effect
	•	A cognitive bias where an observer’s overall impression of a person influences thoughts or feelings about their specific traits.

Introduction to Key OBE Frameworks
Two of the most influential figures in Outcome-Based Education (OBE) are Dr. William Spady and Prof. John Biggs. Both provided essential frameworks that shaped how OBE is applied in schools and higher education today.

Dr. William Spady
	•	Known as the "Father of Outcome-Based Education".
	•	An American sociologist and education reformer.
	•	He introduced Transformational OBE, a model that focuses on long-term outcomes that students need for life—beyond academics.
	•	For Spady, OBE is about preparing students to fulfill real-life roles such as becoming responsible citizens, productive workers, or even future parents.
	•	His OBE is learner-centered, emphasizes clarity of purpose, and aims to ensure that all students succeed with the right support.







Prof. John Biggs
	•	An Australian educational psychologist and scholar in higher education.
	•	He developed OBTL (Outcome-Based Teaching and Learning) with Catherine Tang.
	•	Biggs' model translates OBE into classroom practice by aligning teaching methods, learning activities, and assessments with the intended learning outcomes.
	•	He introduced the idea of Constructive Alignment, which ensures that all parts of the learning process (goals, activities, assessments) work together to help students succeed.

Validity
Does the test measure what it is supposed to measure?
Types of Validity 
1. Face Validity – Test looks like it measures what it should (e.g., math test looks like it tests math).

2. Content Validity – Covers all the necessary content or objectives (e.g., science test includes all key topics).

3. Criterion Validity – Compares with other tests:
	•	Concurrent – Matches current test results (e.g., reading test aligns with national exams).
	•	Predictive – Forecasts future outcomes (e.g., entrance exam predicts college success).

4. Construct Validity – Measures a specific psychological trait:
	•	Convergent – Matches similar traits (e.g., critical thinking aligns with logic test).
	•	Divergent – Doesn’t overlap with unrelated traits (e.g., creativity test doesn’t match memory test).

Reliability
Are the test results consistent across time, forms, or items?
Methods of Measuring Reliability 
1. Test-Retest – Checks stability over time (same test given twice to the same grou)

2. Parallel Forms – Checks consistency between two similar tests (uses two equivalent versions)

3. Split-Half – Checks internal consistency.
Compares scores from two halves of the same test (e.g., odd vs even items).

4. Kuder-Richardson (KR-20) – Checks consistency across items in objective tests (uses a formula for multiple-choice reliability)

Difficulty Index (D.I.)
	•	What it means: Shows how easy or hard a test item is.
	•	How it works: The higher the % of correct answers, the easier the item.

	•	0.00 – 0.20 → Very Difficult

	•	0.41 – 0.60 → Moderate
	•	0.61 – 0.80 → Easy
	•	0.81 – 1.00 → Very Easy
Example: If only 3 out of 10 students got it right, D.I. = 0.30 (Difficult)



Discrimination Index
	•	What it means: Shows if a test item can tell the difference between high and low performers.
	•	How it works: Compares scores of top students and bottom students.
	•	Interpretation:
	•	Close to +1 → Very good at discriminating
	•	Around 0 → Poor discrimination
	•	Negative → Bad item; may be confusing or flawed
Example: If high scorers get the item right and low scorers get it wrong → Good discrimination

What Is Experiential Learning?
 Experiential learning means learning through real-life experience rather than just through books or lectures. It involves doing, reflecting, and applying what you've learned.

According to David Kolb (1984):
“Learning is the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience.”
Philosophical Roots
 1. Pragmatism
	•	Belief that ideas must be tested through practice.
	•	If it works → it’s true/good; if not → it’s false/bad.
	•	Also known as:
	•	Experimentalism – (Charles Sanders Peirce)
	•	Practicalism – (William James)
	•	Instrumentalism – (John Dewey)
Example: A new teaching strategy is only valuable if it helps students learn better in practice.
2. Progressivism
	•	Belief that education must constantly evolve.
	•	Education is the reconstruction of experience.
	•	Change (not permanence) is the core of reality.
Example: Adapting teaching methods as student needs and society change.

	•	Action Research is research done by teachers for themselves. (Mertler, 2009)

	•	Ethics of Responsibility – Researchers must commit to principled actions
