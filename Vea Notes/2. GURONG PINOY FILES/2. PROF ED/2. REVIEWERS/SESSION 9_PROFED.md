Cognitive Level 
Description (Definition for LET Exam)

1. Remembering
Retrieving, recognizing, or recalling factual information, terms, and basic concepts from long-term memory. It is the lowest level of learning.
Define, list, recall, name, identify, state, memorize, recognize
"What is the capital of the Philippines?" or identifying specific dates in history.
2. Understanding
Constructing meaning and grasping the concept. It goes beyond rote memorization by allowing a student to explain, interpret, or summarize ideas.
Explain, summarize, interpret, classify, discuss, paraphrase
Summarizing the main theme of a story or explaining a teaching theory in your own words.
3. Applying
Using information, rules, or procedures in new, concrete, and real-life situations. It is about putting knowledge into practice.
Apply, execute, implement, solve, use, compute, demonstrate
Calculating a student's final grade using a specific formula or applying a psychological theory to a classroom management problem.
4. Analyzing
Breaking information down into its component parts to understand how they relate. It involves examining, categorizing, and finding evidence.
Analyze, compare, contrast, differentiate, outline, deconstruct
Comparing two different philosophies of education (e.g., Idealism vs. Pragmatism) or identifying bias in a text.
5. Evaluating
Making judgments based on criteria, standards, or values. It requires students to critique, justify, or defend a decision.
Evaluate, critique, judge, justify, defend, assess, verify
Critiquing a lesson plan based on specific pedagogical standards or defending a thesis statement.
6. Creating
The highest level in the revised taxonomy. It involves reorganizing elements into a new, original pattern, product, or idea.
Create, design, formulate, invent, compose, construct, develop
Designing a new instructional material, planning a unique research study, or writing an original poem.

13. A teacher applies Jerome Bruner's spiral curriculum in designing her lessons. Which of the following practices best reflects this approach?
	•	Teaching a concept once and moving on to a completely new topic
	•	Grouping students by ability and teaching each group different content
	•	Revisiting the same concepts across grade levels with increasing complexity and depth
	•	Using only discovery learning for all subject areas

Bruner's spiral curriculum is an educational design framework where students revisit the same core topics multiple times throughout their schooling, with each return increasing in complexity, depth, and sophistication.

Bruner’s Three Modes of Representation: Bruner argued that learners process information in three distinct stages. It starts as enactive (hands-on, concrete actions), moves to iconic (visual images, charts, and summaries), and finishes as symbolic (abstract language, codes, formulas, and logic). A spiral curriculum moves students through these exact modes over time.

Piaget explained that children organize their knowledge into mental building blocks called schemas (frameworks for understanding things). 

	•	Assimilation: Fitting new information directly into an existing schema. (e.g., A child sees a raccoon for the first time and calls it a "cat" because it has fur and four legs).

	•	Accommodation: Modifying an existing schema or creating a completely new one because the old framework does not fit the new information. (e.g., Realizing the animal does not meow, has a masked face, and acts differently, so they create a new "raccoon" category).

	•	Equilibrium: The state of mental balance when a child's schemas match up perfectly with the real-world environment around them. When a contradiction happens, they enter disequilibrium (frustration), which forces them to learn and adapt.
