BASIC SENTENCE PATTERNS

	•	Subject – Intransitive Verb
S: Subject (the performer of the action)
IV: Intransitive Verb (action verb that needs no direct object)

Example: The sun rises.
                         S     IV

Tip: To spot an intransitive verb, ask "Who?" or "What?" right after the verb. If the question doesn't make sense or has no answer in the sentence, the verb needs no direct object, meaning it is intransitive.

Example: The sun rises (what?) (makes no sense)
                 The sun rises (who?) (makes no sense)

	•	Subject + Linking Verb + Predicate Adjective/Noun
S: Subject (the performer of the action)
LV: Linking Verb (connects the subject to more information)
C: Complement (completes the meaning of the subject)
	•	PA: Predicate Adjective (adjective that describes the subject)
	•	PN: Predicate Noun / Nominative (noun that renames the subject)

Example: The soup smells delicious.                      You are a teacher.
                         S        LV      C (PA)                            S    LV      C(PN)

Note: To spot a linking verb, replace it with an equal sign (=) or "is/are." If the sentence still makes sense and the subject equals the description, it is a linking verb.

Example: The soup = delicious (still makes sense)
                You are a teacher / You = a teacher (still makes sense)

	•	Subject + Transitive Verb + Direct Object
S: Subject (the performer of the action)
TV: Transitive Verb (an action verb that requires a direct object) (kabaliktaran ni IV)
DO: Direct Object (the person or thing receiving the action of the verb)

Example: Alex writes poems.
                   S     TV       DO

Note: To spot a transitive verb, ask "What?" or "Who?" right after the verb. If the sentence answers that question, the verb is transitive, and that answer is your direct object.

Example: Alex writes (what?) poems. (the question what is answered with direct object)
	•	Subject + Transitive Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object
S: Subject (the performer of the action)
TV: Transitive Verb (an action verb that requires a direct object) (kabaliktaran ni IV)
DO: Direct Object (the person or thing receiving the action of the verb)
IO: Indirect Object (the person or thing to whom or for whom the action is done)

Example: I gave my friend a gift.
               S   TV         IO         DO

Note: To find the indirect object, ask "To whom?" or "For whom?" about the direct object. The person or thing that answers this question is the indirect object.

Example: I gave my friend a gift. (For whom is the gift? For my friend)

	•	Subject + Transitive Verb + Direct Object + Object of Preposition
S: Subject (the performer of the action)
TV: Transitive Verb (an action verb that requires a direct object) (kabaliktaran ni IV)
DO: Direct Object (the person or thing receiving the action of the verb)
OP: Object of the Preposition (the noun or pronoun that follows a preposition)

Example: I wrote a poem under the tree.
               S   TV        DO               OP

Note: To find the preposition and its object, ask "Where?" or "When?" right after the direct object. The location or time phrase that answers this will contain the preposition and the object of the preposition.

Example I wrote a poem (where?) under the tree. (question is answered)

	•	Subject + Transitive Verb + Direct Object + Object Complement
S: Subject (the performer of the action)
TV: Transitive Verb (an action verb that requires a direct object) (kabaliktaran ni IV)
DO: Direct Object (the person or thing receiving the action of the verb)
OC: Object Complement (a word that modifies, describes, or renames the Direct Object)

Example: The teacher made the lesson interesting.
                         S             TV        DO              OC            

Note: The object complement always comes right after the direct object. To spot it, ask "What?" or "How?" about the direct object to see what it became or how it is being described.

Example: The teacher made the lesson (what?) interesting. (question is answered)

TYPES OF SENTENCES (STRUCTURE)

Independent Clause (IC)
An independent clause is a group of words that contains a subject and a verb and expresses a complete thought.

Dependent Clause (DC)
A dependent clause is a group of words that contains a subject and a verb but does not express a complete thought.

Coordinating Conjunction (CC)
A coordinating conjunction is a word used to connect words, phrases, or independent sentences that are grammatically equal in importance. (FANBOYS)

Subordinating Conjunction (SC)
A subordinating conjunction is a word that introduces a dependent clause and glues it to an independent sentence.

SUBJECT VERB AGREEMENT

Rules of S-V-Agreement
1. The orchestra plays a hit song.
Rule: Collective nouns (like orchestra) take a singular verb when the group acts as a single, unified unit performing the same action together.

2. The team are arguing among themselves.
Rule: Collective nouns (like team) take a plural verb when the individuals within the group are acting separately or are in conflict (indicated by the clue among themselves).

3. The information is relevant to my study.
Rule: Non-countable/mass nouns (like information) are always grammatically singular and require a singular verb.

4. Three sacks of rice are heavy.
Rule: When a quantity phrase has an "of" modifier, the verb agrees with the countable head noun (sacks, which is plural) rather than the mass noun inside the phrase (rice).

5. The news was fake.
Rule: The word news looks plural because it ends in "-s", but it is an uncountable noun that is strictly singular in construction.

6. Pliers are used for electrical work.
Rule: Tools, instruments, or articles of clothing consisting of two parts joined together (like pliers, scissors, pants) are inherently plural and take plural verbs unless preceded by the phrase "a pair of".

7. Six plus six equals twelve.
Rule: Mathematical operations and equations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) are treated as singular units and take a singular verb.

8. Everything happens for a reason.
Rule: Indefinite pronouns ending in -thing, -one, or -body (like everything, someone, anybody) are always grammatically singular and require a singular verb.

9. Few participants are expected to convene after workshop.
Rule: The indefinite pronouns few, both, many, several, and others are always plural and require a plural verb.

10. None of them avoid the traffic.
Rule: When using none of followed by a plural countable noun or pronoun (them), a plural verb is typically used in modern English and standardized board exams because it refers to multiple individuals within that group.

11. One half of the information is irrelevant.
Rule: For fractions, percentages, or partitive expressions, the verb agrees with the noun following the word "of". Since information is a non-countable noun, it takes a singular verb.

12. The Malaya creek or the surrounding rivers are sources of plastic wastes.
Rule: When two subjects are connected by or or nor, the verb must agree with the subject closest to it. Since the surrounding rivers is plural, the verb must be plural.

13. Not only the father but also the siblings are also in opposition to this.
Rule: When subjects are joined by correlative conjunctions like not only... but also, the verb must agree with the subject closest to it. Since the siblings is plural, the verb is plural.

14. Here come my friends.
Rule: In sentences beginning with here or there (expletives/inverted word order), the true subject follows the verb. Since my friends is a plural subject, it takes the plural verb come.

15. Bacon and egg is my favorite breakfast.
Rule: When two nouns joined by and represent a single combined idea, entity, or dish (indicated here by the singular phrase my favorite breakfast), they take a singular verb.

16. It is the leader, not his members, that has opened the plenary.
Rule: The relative pronoun that refers back to its true antecedent, the leader (singular). Intervening parenthetical phrases set off by commas (like not his members) do not affect the number of the true subject.

17. The student, together with her classmates, studies the lessons well.
Rule: Parenthetical expressions introduced by words like together with, along with, as well as, or accompanied by do not alter the number of the subject. The true subject is The student (singular), so the verb must be singular.

18. The rich are getting richer.
Rule: When an adjective is preceded by the article the to represent a whole class or group of people (such as the rich, the poor, the young), it functions as a plural noun and requires a plural verb.
