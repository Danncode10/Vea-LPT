Related Concepts to Know
	•	Bajo de la Campana: Literally means "under the sound of the bell." This was the literal rule of Reduccion. If you could hear the church bells, you lived close enough to be controlled and monitored.
	•	Visita: Small, outlying villages surrounding the cabecera. These smaller settlements did not have a resident priest but were visited occasionally by friars for mass and sacraments.
	•	Plaza Complex: The physical layout of the pueblo. It placed the church, the town hall (tribunal), and houses of elite locals directly around a central open square to visualize Spanish authority.
	•	Principalia: The noble class of native Filipinos. This group was made up of current and former local officials like gobernadorcillos and cabezas de barangay who enjoyed special tax privileges.
	•	Bandala: A forced economic system where indigenous farmers were required to sell their agricultural products exclusively to the Spanish government at artificially low, fixed prices.

6. What is the first book published in the Philippines?
	•	Barlaane Josaphat
	•	Pasiong Mahal
	•	Doctrina Chritiana
	•	Del Superior Govierno

The Doctrina Christiana (The Christian Doctrine) was the very first book printed and published in the Philippines, released in 1593. 
	•	The Author: It was written by the Franciscan friar Fray Juan de Plasencia.
	•	The Content: It served as a Roman Catholic catechism book featuring basic prayers like the Our Father, Hail Mary, and the Ten Commandments.
	•	The Typography: It was printed using xylography (woodblock printing). It is historically unique because the text was published in multiple scripts: Spanish, Romanized Tagalog, and the pre-colonial native script called Baybayin.

9. During the Spanish era, who was the revolutionary leader who waged a long war against the government because it did not allow proper burial for his brother?
	•	Macario Sacay
	•	Diego Silang
	•	Andres Bonifacio
	•	Francisco Dagohoy

Francisco Dagohoy (born Francisco Sendrijas) led the famous Dagohoy Rebellion in Bohol, which lasted for 85 years (1744 to 1829). It holds the record as the longest rebellion in Philippine history.
	•	The Cause: Dagohoy's brother, Sagarbino, died in a duel while executing an arrest order under a Jesuit priest, Father Gaspar Morales.
	•	The Denial: Father Morales refused to give Sagarbino a proper Christian burial because dying in a duel was considered a sin. Outraged that his brother's body was left to rot for days, Dagohoy ignited a massive revolt that established an independent state in the mountains of Bohol.

Carlos Maria de la Torre (1869–1871)
Most Beloved Liberal Governor-General
Sent by a progressive Spanish government, he abolished press censorship, stopped the physical flogging of native soldiers, and treated Filipinos as equals. This brief "breathing room" directly inspired the Ilustrados and native priests to actively push for national reforms.
Narciso Claveria (1844–1849)
The Standardizer of Filipino Surnames
He issued the famous 1849 Claveria Decree, producing the Catálogo Alfabético de Apellidos (Alphabetical Catalogue of Surnames). He forced early Filipinos to choose formal Spanish surnames to systematically improve census data, fix chaotic records, and streamline tax collection.
Jose Basco y Vargas (1778–1787)
The Economic Modernizer
He established a massive General Economic Plan to make the Philippines self-sufficient and financially independent of Mexico. He launched the profitable but highly exploitative Tobacco Monopoly (1782) and officially annexed the isolated Batanes islands.
Rafael Izquierdo (1871–1873)
The Executioner of GOMBURZA
Directly replacing the liberal De la Torre, he ruled with an iron fist and immediately dismantled all progressive reforms. He used the 1872 Cavite Mutiny as a political excuse to implicate and execute the three secular martyr priests: Gomez, Burgos, and Zamora (GOMBURZA).
Eulogio Despujol (1891–1893)
The Exiler of Jose Rizal
Initially presenting himself as a reform-minded leader to gain local trust, his legacy was cemented when he ordered the immediate arrest and exile of Dr. Jose Rizal to Dapitan in July 1892. This direct suppression of peaceful reform caused Bonifacio to launch the militant Katipunan (KKK) that very same week.
Camilo de Polavieja (1896–1897)
The Executioner of Jose Rizal
A brutal, highly militaristic general sent specifically to crush the raging 1896 Philippine Revolution. He officially confirmed and signed the death warrant leading to the execution of Dr. Jose Rizal on December 30, 1896, instantly turning Rizal into the ultimate national martyr.

15. The law that led to the granting of ten-year transition period is called:
	•	Hare-Hawes Cutting Bill
	•	Tydings-McDuffie Law
	•	Jones Law
	•	Bell Trade Act

The Tydings-McDuffie Act (officially known as the Philippine Independence Act of 1934) is the exact law that successfully established the 10-year transition government known as the Commonwealth of the Philippines.
	•	The Mission: After the Philippine Senate rejected an earlier bill, Manuel L. Quezon led a new independence mission to Washington, D.C..
	•	The Result: He secured this law, which was signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on March 24, 1934. It successfully paved the way for the drafting of the 1935 Constitution and guaranteed absolute independence, which was ultimately realized on July 4, 1946.
