What error is committed in the sentence: I was tired I went to sleep.
	•	run-on
	•	faulty parallelism
	•	dangling modifier
	•	comma splice
I was tired, so I went to sleep.
I was tired, so I went to sleep.
Ex: I was tired (___) I went to sleep.
	•	A run-on sentence occurs when two independent clauses (complete thoughts) are joined together without any punctuation or coordinating conjunctions.
I was tired; I went to sleep.
I was tired; I went to sleep.
Ex: I was tired, I went to sleep.
	•	A comma splice occurs when you join two independent clauses (complete sentences) together with only a comma.
My hobbies include reading, writing, and analyzing literature.
My hobbies include reading, writing, and analyzing literature.
Ex: My hobbies include reading, writing, and to analyze literature.
	•	Faulty parallelism occurs when a sentence mixes different grammatical forms in a series, list, or comparison.
I found a woman’s silver bracelet.
I found a woman’s silver bracelet.
Ex: I found a silver woman’s bracelet.
	•	I told my brother I would play with him when the class was over.
I told my brother I would play with him when the class was over.
When the class was over, I told my brother I would play with him.
When the class was over, I told my brother I would play with him.

Ex: I told my brother when the class was over I would play with him.
	•	A squinting modifier is a very specific type of misplaced modifier. It happens when a descriptive phrase is placed right in the middle of two actions, making it look both left and right. Because it sits in the middle, the reader cannot tell which action it is supposed to describe.
After finishing the homework, I turned on the TV.
After finishing the homework, I turned on the TV.
Ex: After finishing the homework, the TV was turned on.
	•	A dangling modifier occurs when a descriptive phrase is included in a sentence, but the person or thing doing the action is completely missing from the sentence.

FIGURES OF SPEECH
Figure of Speech
Definition
Example
Simile

An indirect comparison that explicitly uses the words 'as' or 'like'.




She is as brave as a lion. / He runs like the wind.

Metaphor




A direct comparison between two unrelated things without using 'as' or 'like'.




Time is a thief. / You are my sunshine.
Personification




Giving human qualities, feelings, or actions to non-human things or objects.






The wind whispered through the trees.
Litotes





An understatement using a negative statement to express a positive meaning.

You are not bad. (meaning you are quite good)
Apostrophe






Directly addressing an absent person, abstract idea, or object as if it were present.





O Death, where is thy sting?
Allusion



An indirect reference to a well-known person, place, event, or literary work.




She is the Helen in our group. (referencing Helen of Troy)
Hyperbole


An extreme exaggeration used for emphasis, not meant to be taken literally.






I've told you a million times to clean your room!
Irony

A situation where the actual outcome is the opposite of what is logically expected.






A fire station burning down.
Synecdoche







A figure of speech based on a part-to-whole (or whole-to-part) relationship.





All hands on deck. (hands = crew members)
Metonymy

Replacing a word or concept with something closely related to it.






The pen is mightier than the sword. (pen = written word)
Paradox





A statement that seems self-contradictory but reveals a deeper truth.

The only constant in life is change.
Oxymoron




Two contradictory or opposite terms placed directly side by side.





Cruel kindness, Open secret, Deafening silence
Bathos

A sudden shift from a serious or lofty tone to something trivial or ridiculous.





He spent his final hour of life doing what he loved most: arguing with his wife.
Hypophora





A figure of speech where a speaker raises a question and then immediately answers it themselves.





Nagtanong ka tapos sinagot mo rin agad. (e.g., Should we give up? Absolutely not.)
Malapropism
The mistaken use of an incorrect word in place of a similar-sounding word, often with an amusing result.





He's the pineapple of politeness. (instead of pinnacle)
Spoonerism




An error in speech where the initial sounds of two or more words are switched.





I love chork pop. Belly jeans (instead of pork chop, jelly beans)
Rhetorical Question

A question asked merely for effect or to make a point, with no answer expected.






A question that doesn't need an answer. (e.g., Are you crazy?)
Understatement

Presenting something as less important, smaller, or less urgent than it actually is.






Less than it is. (e.g., It's just a scratch when the car is totaled).
Euphemism






A mild, indirect, or pleasant word used to substitute for an unpleasant, harsh, or offensive one.






Your dog has gone to a better place. (instead of saying it died)
Dysphemism




A harsh, blunt, or offensive word used instead of a neutral or mild one.






Your dog died. or Croaked (the opposite of a euphemism)

In a tragic drama, the hero’s downfall evokes strong feelings of pity and fear in the audience, resulting in an emotional release or purification at the end of the play. This emotional effect is known as _______.
	•	Catharsis
	•	Peripeteia
	•	Hamartia
	•	hubris

	•	Catharsis: An Aristotlean term describing the emotional release, cleansing, or purification experienced by the audience at the end of a tragedy. By witnessing the intense pity and fear on stage, the audience leaves feeling emotionally refreshed and balanced.

	•	Peripeteia: A sudden reversal of fortune or an unexpected turning point in a story, particularly in a tragedy where things pivot from good to catastrophically bad for the hero.

	•	Hamartia: The tragic hero's fatal flaw or error in judgment that inevitably sets their tragic downfall into motion (e.g., Romeo's impulsiveness).

	•	Hubris: A very specific and common type of hamartia, referring to excessive, blind pride or arrogance that causes a character to defy the gods or natural order.

	•	Choka: A traditional form of long Japanese poetry consisting of alternating lines of 5 and 7 syllables, ending with an extra line of 7 syllables. It has no fixed limit on the total number of lines.

	•	Tanka: A short five-line Japanese poem with a fixed syllable structure of 5-7-5-7-7, totaling 31 syllables. It is the older, parent form from which the haiku evolved.

	•	Renga: A collaborative or linked-verse Japanese poetic form where multiple poets alternate writing stanzas (typically alternating between 3-line and 2-line links) to build a long chain poem.

	•	Haiku: A short three-line Japanese poetic form with a strict 5-7-5 syllable pattern totaling 17 syllables. It traditionally requires a focus on a natural setting or season.

	•	Flashback story: A narrative technique where the chronological timeline is interrupted to show an event that happened in the past, usually to give background context about a character.
