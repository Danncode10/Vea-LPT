CONTENT KNOWLEDGE AND PEDAGOGY

The mastery of content and comprehensive pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) of the subject area is one of the competencies expected in a teacher education graduate (CHED 2017). 

	3 Categories of Teacher’s Knowledge:
	•	Content Knowledge – the knowledge of the subject matter or academic course to be taught or expected to be learned by students, which requires understanding of facts, concepts, or principles
	•	Pedagogical knowledge – the knowledge of teaching, which includes methods and strategies of formulating the subject matter to make it more comprehensible to the learners
	•	Curricular knowledge – the knowledge of the curriculum, which is represented by full range of programs designed for teaching a particular subject/course at a given level
PCK – blending of content knowledge and pedagogical knowledge that is unique among others. It is the type of knowledge in which the teacher relates his/her pedagogical knowledge to the content knowledge to make the lesson relevant and comprehensible to learners.

Notes: 
	•	Higher-order Thinking Skills (HOTS) is a concept of learning based on cognitive processes that are beyond the basic thinking skills classified in Bloom’s Taxonomy.
	•	HOTS are analysis (differentiating, organizing, attributing), synthesis (checking, critiquing), and evaluation (hypothesizing, designing, constructing)
	•	Critical and creative thinking are considered to be HOTS as they require a more complex level of thinking. Critical thinking is the ability to make logical, rational, and reasonable judgement using HOTS such as analyzing, synthesizing, and evaluating. Creating thinking is the ability to become resourceful, open-minded, innovative, and adaptive in addressing problems involving HOTS such as analyzing, designing, composing, and constructing to generate ideas or create something new.

Some strategies to enhance HOTS:
	•	Teach language and concept of higher-order thinking
	•	Encourage questioning and discussion to tap into particular HOTS
	•	Teach subject concepts to connect students
	•	Provide scaffolding by giving student support at the beginning of the lesson such as visuals, graphic organizers, and problem-solving tasks
	•	Encourage higher-order thinking to foster deep conceptual understanding

Teachers Use of HOTS Strategies
	•	Teaches the concepts of HOTS
	•	Promotes the use of HOTS when communicating
	•	Encourages question and answer during discussion
	•	Uses visuals and graphic organizers to connect concepts

	•	Promotes collaborative learning to generate new learning
	•	Encourages students to elaborate their answers
	•	Promotes inquiry-based learning to validate ideas

Micro level – curriculum alignment ensures that the learning outcomes are aligned with learning competencies prescribed in the curriculum. ASSESSMENT AND REPORTING

	•	Assessment is a continuous process of identifying, gathering, organizing, and interpreting quantitative and qualitative information about what learners know and can do. 

3 ways to assess students learning according to purpose:
	•	 Diagnostic assessment  is given at the beginning of the school year or at the beginning of the new unit of study to quantify what the students already know.
	•	Formative assessment is given throughout the learning process to determine how students are progressing through a certain learning goal.
	•	Summative assessment is given at the end of the year or unit to assess the mastery of the topic after instruction.

	•	Learning is a two-way process. Learners must be given feedback about their performance. The following is a checklist for giving feedback to students (Gibbs et al. 1986)
	•	Keep the time short between the student writing and the feedback. 
	•	Substantiate a grade or mark with comments both in text for specific aspects and with summary at the end.
	•	Balance negative comments with positive ones and ensure that negative ones are constructive.
	•	Follow-up written comments with oral feedback and aim for a dialogue.
	•	Make the criteria clear to students when setting the work and give them written criteria where possible.
	•	Make further suggestions to further develop ideas.
	•	Give periodic oral feedback on rough drafts.

Checklist for giving of feedback:
	•	The student feedback is educative in nature.
	•	The student feedback is given in a timely manner.
	•	The teacher is sensitive to the individual needs of the students.
	•	The teacher anticipates the four questions. (What can the students do? What can’t the student do? How does the student’s work compare with that of others? How can the student do better?)
	•	The student feedback is a reference for a skill or specific knowledge.
	•	The teacher gives feedback to keep the students “on target” for achievement.
	•	The teacher hosts a one-o-one conference.
	•	The student feedback is given verbally, non-verbally or in written form.
	•	The teacher concentrates on one ability.
	•	The teacher alternates due dates for students.
	•	The teacher educates the students on how to give feedback to each other.
	•	The teacher asks another adult to give student feedback.
	•	The students take down notes on teacher’s verbal feedback.
	•	The teacher uses a notebook to keep track of student progress.
	•	The teacher returns tests, papers, or comment cards at the beginning of the class.
	•	The teacher uses Post-It notes in giving written feedback.
	•	The teacher gives genuine praise.
	•	The teacher uses the phrase “ I noticed…” in acknowledging the student’s effort.
	•	The teacher provides a model or an example.
	•	The teacher invites students to give YOU feedback.

PERSONAL GROWTH AND PROFSSIONAL DEVELOPMENT

	•	Teaching philosophies can be categorized into two:
	•	Teacher-centered philosophy underscores the relevance of teachers in education. This places an emphasis on the teachers’ roles in transmitting knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values.
	•	Essentialism advocates the teaching of basic skills in order to train the mind. Core knowledge in different disciplines is essential and the teachers should use lectures, memorization, drills, and assessment.
	•	Perennialism focuses on developing critical thinking skills, mastery of content in a sequential order and reasoning skills.
	•	Learner-centered philosophy puts emphasis on how students learn. Teachers who adhere to this philosophy focuses on maximizing the potential of the students in order to equip them with knowledge, skills, and attitudes that will help them face and overcome the challenges of the real world. 
	•	Progressivism believes in equipping students with the ability to learn through hands-on engagement. Small group discussions and learning centers dominate the progressive classroom.
	•	Existentialism believes in giving the students opportunities to learn concepts that interest them. Teachers believe that students should be to find meaning in whatever it is that they are learning.
	•	Social reconstructionism believes that schools can contribute to solving social problems. Teachers engage students in projects or activities that will enable them to address a social issue and create an impact in society.

INSTRUCTIONAL PLANNING

NOTES:
	•	Instructional objectives are statements that will describe what the learner will be able to do after completing the instruction.

Characteristics of instructional objectives:
	•	Instructional objectives are specific, measurable, short term, observable student behaviors. They indicate the desirable knowledge, skills, or attitudes to be gained.
	•	An instructional objective is the focal point of a lesson plan. Objectives are the foundation upon which you can build lessons and assessments and instruction that you can prove meet your overall course or lesson goals.
	•	Think of objectives as tools you use to make sure you reach your goals. They are the arrows you shoot towards your target (goal).
	•	Begin with an understanding of the particular content to which the objectives will relate.
	•	The purpose of objectives is not to restrict spontaneity or constrain the vision of education in the discipline; but to ensure that learning is focused clearly enough that both students and teacher know what is going on, and so learning can be objectively measured. 

	•	Appropriate teaching strategies that are aligned with the instructional objectives and assessments are vital elements in the success of any classroom instruction. There are some guiding principles in the selection and usage of appropriate teaching strategies (Corpuz & Salandanan, 2013).
	•	Learning is an active process.
	•	The more senses that are involved in learning, the more and better learning.
	•	Emotion has the power to increase retention and learning.
	•	Learning is meaningful when it is connected to students’ everyday life.
	•	Good teaching goes beyond the recall of information.
	•	An integral teaching approach is far more effective than teaching isolated bits of information.
	•	In preparing appropriate instructional materials, the use of all five senses is very significant. There are guiding principles in using IMs (Corpuz & Salandanan, 2013).
	•	All IMs are aids to instruction. They do not replace the teacher.
	•	Choose the IM that best suits your instructional objectives 
	•	If possible, use a variety of tools.
	•	Check out your IM before class starts to be sure it is working properly.
