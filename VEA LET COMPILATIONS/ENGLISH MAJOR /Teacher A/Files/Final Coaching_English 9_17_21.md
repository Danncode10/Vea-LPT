Different types of newspaper articles:

• News Reports
     - found at the front of a newspaper
     - inform readers about things that are happening in the world
        or in the local area.
     - full of facts, like names, dates and places
     - tend to have a more formal, neutral tone.
• Feature articles
       - explore the issues raised by news stories in more depth
       - tends to be more opinionated and less formal than a report
       - often taking a personal point of view.
Different types of newspaper articles:

• Editorials, columns and opinion pieces
       - pieces by 'personality' writers, often celebrities in other
         fields
       - written to inform (because the writer's expert opinion is
         valued), to entertain (because the writer has a comic or
         interesting way of describing everyday life).
       - Columnists develop a style of their own – for
         example, polemical or sarcastic. They create this style
         through vocabulary choices and rhetorical devices.
       - might use informal language to convey their idiolect. This
         individual style appeals to their audience.

             10 Frequently Used News Angles

1.New Development - Brings forward new information to an
  existing story or exposes an entirely original news story.
2. Human Interest - Relates the story to social issues or
   discusses a person in an emotional way, as to generate interest
   or empathy in the reader.
3. Local Angle - Describes how the local community or economy
   will be affected.
4. Progress - Demonstrates human innovation, positive
   interactions, or willingness to meet the challenge presented.
5. Consequence - Relates a group or organization to an existing
    news event, usually describing how it's being affected by a
   previous announcement or occurrence.
              10 Frequently Used News Angles

6. Eminence or Prominence - Discusses emerging trends
   (usually informing the reader of why the trend is popular and
   what it means to them).
7. Conflict - Explains a controversy often with opposing view
   points and positions.
8. Drama - Describes a conflict likely to invoke an emotional
   response or provides an editorial of such events.
9. Disaster - Describes the impact of negative situations (and
   usually either what brought them about, how it's affecting the
   new subject, or what's being done about it).
10. Timing and Proximity - Relates a particular story (often
   warnings or advice) to a specific region or event (such as a
   holiday or season).

1. “Bandwagon Approach”
  “It must be cool because everyone is doing it…
  “90 percent of computer users choose Macs”
  (sometimes called Appeal to Popularity)

2.“Slippery Slope” - a fallacious argument built on the
 supposition
   that a small step will lead to a larger chain of events
  “If you don’t stop smoking, then you are going to start shooting
   heroin.”
  “Marijuana is the gateway drug.”

3 .“False Authority” - using a celebrity or authority figure’s
 name to support an issue which if not really his or her
 expertise.
     “Katie Perry thinks the killer should be pardoned.”
4. Hasty Generalization - type of inductive reasoning –
 conclusions made through insufficient evidence
  “The last two mass murderers were from Michigan. Obviously,
 people from Michigan are dangerous.”

5. Begging the question - A is true because A is true . . .
 “chocolate is healthy because it is good for you”

6. Straw Man - a fallacy of distraction – an irrelevant conclusion
  “How could he possibly cheat on his taxes? He’s such a great
   father!”

7. Ad misericordiam (Appeal to Pity) - appealing to someone’s
 emotions in order to distract them from the truth.
8. Ad Hominem – pointing out a negative characteristic to support
 one’s argument
  “How could Mary know anything about cars? She’s a woman!

9. Either/Or fallacy aka False Dilemma - false dichotomy
   “You’re either part of the solution or part of the problem.”

10. Post Hoc - the arguer infers that because one event follows
 another, the first event must be the cause of the second event.
 Causation without correlation
  “The rooster crowed, the sun came up. Therefore, the rooster made
 the sun come up.”
