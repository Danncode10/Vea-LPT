	•	Speech Act Theory ni J.L. Austin ang nagsabing “saying something is also doing something” — kaya hindi lang basta salita, kundi aksyon na rin.
	•	Ayon kay Austin, may tatlong uri ng acts sa isang utterance: locutionary, illocutionary, at perlocutionary.
	•	Locutionary act is yung literal na pagsabi ng words — like “Ang init naman.”
	•	Illocutionary act ay yung intended function ng sinabi mo — like baka gustong buksan ng kausap mo ang bintana.
	•	Perlocutionary act ay yung actual effect ng sinabi mo sa listener — for example, napa-on niya ang electric fan dahil sabi mo mainit.
	•	Si John Searle ay nag-expand ng Speech Act Theory ni Austin; siya ang nag-categorize ng mga speech acts.
	•	Ayon kay Searle, may five types of speech acts: assertive, directive, commissive, expressive, at declarative.
	•	Assertive speech act expresses belief — like “Uulan bukas.”
	•	Directive speech act is kapag gusto mong may gawin ang kausap — gaya ng “Pakikuha naman ng tubig.”
	•	Commissive speech act ay pangako or commitment — gaya ng “Babalikan kita.”
	•	Expressive speech act shows feelings — tulad ng “Pasensya na sa abala.”
	•	Declarative speech act ay nagbabago ng estado ng isang bagay — like “Pinapatawan ka ng suspensyon.”
	•	Kapag ang salita mismo ang gumagawa ng aksyon (ex: "I now declare the meeting open"), that's a performative utterance.
	•	May mga felicity conditions para maging effective ang speech act — kailangan tama ang context, speaker, at intention.
	•	Example: Hindi valid ang “I now pronounce you husband and wife” kung hindi pari o judge ang nagsabi.
	•	Indirect speech act ay yung sinasabi mo sa paraang di-direkta, pero may ibang ibig sabihin — like “Ang dilim dito ah…” meaning gusto mo ng ilaw.
	•	Sa Filipino culture, indirect speech acts are common para maging magalang o iwas confrontation.
	•	Speech acts work differently sa high-context cultures tulad ng Pilipinas, dahil maraming meaning ang galing sa context, hindi lang sa words.
	•	Ang constative utterance ay pahayag na pwedeng totoo o hindi — like “Mainit sa Maynila.”
	•	Ang performative utterance, on the other hand, ay yung pagsasalita mismo ang gumaganap ng aksyon — like “Sorry.”

	•	In-text Citation Chicago (footnote) – Delos Reyes, Title, 21.
	•	Quoting – Using exact words from a source with quotation marks
	•	Paraphrasing – Restating the author's idea using your own words
	•	Summarizing – Giving the main idea in a shortened form
	•	Plagiarism – Copying without crediting the original author
	•	Signal Phrase – Words introducing a source like "According to Cruz (2022)..."
	•	Reference List (APA) – Alphabetical, double-spaced, hanging indent
	•	Works Cited (MLA) – Alphabetical, double-spaced, hanging indent
	•	Bibliography (Chicago) – Complete list of all sources consulted, not just cited
	•	Et al. – Used when citing more than 3 authors in APA (e.g., Santos et al., 2020)
	•	Ibid. – Used in Chicago footnotes to refer to the same source cited above
	•	DOI – Digital Object Identifier, unique link for academic sources
	•	Research Gap – Unanswered question in the existing studies
	•	Footnote – Notes at the bottom of a page for citation or explanation
	•	Endnote – Notes listed at the end of a document
	•	Visual Citation – Giving credit to images or charts used
	•	Interview Citation APA – Personal communication, not in reference list
	•	Interview Citation MLA – Lastname, Firstname. Personal interview. Day Month Year.
	•	Case Study – Detailed examination of a single subject
	•	Action Research – Problem-solving approach used by practitioners
	•	Experimental Research – Involves manipulation of variables
	•	Descriptive Research – Describes characteristics or phenomena
	•	Correlational Research – Examines relationships between variables
	•	Independent Variable – Variable that is changed or manipulated
	•	Dependent Variable – Outcome being measured
	•	Reliability – Consistency of measurement
	•	Validity – Accuracy of measurement
	•	Triangulation – Using multiple sources or methods for accuracy
	•	Ethical Clearance – Approval for conducting research with people
	•	APA Title Page Format – Title, Name, School, Course, Instructor, Date
	•	MLA Heading Format – Name, Instructor, Course, Date (left-aligned)
	•	Chicago Title Page – Centered title, name, course, and date
	•	T-test – Used to compare the means of two groups (comparing math scores of male vs. female students)
	•	Paired T-test – Used when comparing two related groups (pre-test vs. post-test scores of the same class)
	•	Independent T-test – Used when comparing two separate groups (experimental group vs. control group)
	•	ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) – Used to compare the means of three or more groups (test scores of Grade 7, 8, and 9 students)
	•	One-Way ANOVA – Used for one independent variable with 3+ groups (student performance by learning style)
	•	Two-Way ANOVA – Used for two independent variables with multiple groups (effect of gender and teaching method on scores)
	•	Post Hoc Test (Tukey HSD) – Used after ANOVA to check which specific groups differ (which section scored the highest)
	•	Chi-square Test (χ²) – Used to test relationship between categorical variables (gender vs. favorite school subject)
	•	Pearson r (Correlation Coefficient) – Measures the relationship between two continuous variables (study hours vs. exam score)
	•	Spearman Rho – Measures relationship between ranked/ordinal variables (class rank vs. leadership rating)
	•	Regression Analysis – Used to predict value of one variable using another (predicting score based on study hours)
	•	Linear Regression – Used when the relationship is linear (height and weight comparison)
	•	Multiple Regression – Used when two or more predictors affect the outcome (study hours and attendance to predict GPA)
	•	Z-test – Used to compare means if sample size is large (n > 30) or population variance is known (national average vs. school average)
	•	F-test – Used to compare two variances (variance of male vs. female performance)
	•	Levene’s Test – Tests if variances are equal (checking group similarity before ANOVA)
	•	Mann-Whitney U Test – Non-parametric test for two independent groups (behavior scores of group A vs. group B with skewed data)
	•	Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test – Non-parametric test for two related groups (pre-test and post-test with small data)
	•	Kruskal-Wallis Test – Non-parametric version of One-Way ANOVA (comparing 3 groups with non-normal scores)
	•	Friedman Test – Non-parametric test for repeated measures (testing students across 3 different lesson styles)
	•	Standard Deviation – Shows spread of scores from the mean (small SD = similar scores; large SD = wide spread)
	•	Variance – The square of the standard deviation (if SD = 2, then variance = 4)
	•	Skewness – Tells if distribution is symmetrical or lopsided (scores mostly low = positively skewed)
	•	Kurtosis – Tells if data is peaked or flat (tall peak = high kurtosis)
	•	Null Hypothesis (H₀) – Assumes no difference or effect (H₀: there is no difference in test scores)
	•	Alternative Hypothesis (Hₐ) – Assumes there is a difference (Hₐ: there is a difference in test scores)
	•	Significance Level (α) – Commonly set at 0.05 (if p < 0.05, reject H₀)
	•	P-value – Probability result happened by chance (p = 0.03 means result is significant)
	•	Degrees of Freedom (df) – Related to sample size (in t-test, df = n - 1)
	•	Effect Size – Shows how strong the effect is (large effect size = strong impact)
	•	Cohen’s d – Measures effect size in t-tests (0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large)
	•	r² (Coefficient of Determination) – Shows how much of the change is explained (r² = 0.6 means 60% of variation is explained)
	•	Descriptive Statistics – Summarizes data (mean, median, mode, SD)
	•	Inferential Statistics – Makes predictions or generalizations (t-test, ANOVA, regression)
	•	Parametric Tests – Used for normal distribution and interval/ratio data (t-test, ANOVA)
	•	Non-parametric Tests – Used for non-normal or ordinal data (Mann-Whitney U, Wilcoxon)
