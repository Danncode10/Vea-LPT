                       LITERARY CRITICISM
v The study, evaluation, and interpretation, comparison, analysis and
  commentary of a specific text or texts which have been designated as literature
  or works of literature
v Modern literary criticism is often influenced by literary theory, which is the
  philosophical discussion of literature's goals and methods
v Broaden a reader’s understanding of an author’s work by summarizing,
  interpreting, and exploring its value
v Two primary questions :       a) why does a piece of literature have the precise
                                   characteristics that it has? (how does it work?)
                                b) what is the value of literature?
v Is best understood as the application of a literary theory to specific texts




                                                                     elev8rstraining@gmail.com
              PURPOSE OF LITERARY CRITICISM
• to broaden a reader’s understanding of an author’s work by
  summarizing, interpreting, and exploring its value

• to formulate a comprehensive literary analysis that can inform or
  challenge another reader’s understanding of the text

• creates space for readers to better understand the beauty and
  complexity of the world through literature




                                                          elev8rstraining@gmail.com
                    BENEFITS OF LITERARY CRITICISM
1. Literary criticism expands your worldview. By examining works of literature through different
approaches to literary criticism, you expand your understanding of the world around you. Each
literary style encourages the critic and reader to consider different perspectives from their own.

2. Literary criticism helps you better understand literature. Literary criticism can give you the
tools to study, evaluate, and interpret literary works like novels, short stories, and poems. If you
want to write a critical essay or book review about a particular piece of literature, reading other
examples of literary criticism can help you learn how to frame your point of view.

3. Literary criticism creates opportunities for new styles of writing. With a vast number of
approaches, the practice of literary criticism creates space and context for authors to create
works of literature that push boundaries and break new creative ground.

                            Literary Theory
v Addresses questions that deal with what the language that makes a material
  literary, and the structure of the language and texts, and how these things
  work
v Involves the study of the function of the literary text in social and cultural terms,
  which in turn leads to a construction of its value
v Any literary theory has to account for:
      a) the nature of representation in the text
      b) the nature of reality and its relation to representation
      c) how the representation of reality is accomplished or subverted and denied
      d) the conventions or codes particular writers, literary schools or periods
               might employ to achieve representation

Classical Literary Theory
v premised on the idea that literature is an imitation of life. It is interested in looking
  at literature based on:
  ➢ Mimesis (Plato). Mimesis is the Greek word for imitation. We try to see
  whether a piece of literary work shows imitation of life or reality as we know it. If
  it is, what is imitated? How is the imitation done? Is it a good or bad imitation?

   ➢ Catharsis (Aristotle). Catharsis refers to purgation, purification, clarification, or
   structural kind of emotional cleansing. “Tragedy,” says Aristotle, “is an imitation
   [mimēsis] of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude…
   through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation [catharsis] of these emotions.

Mythological/Archetypal Approach
v Emphasizes “the recurrent universal patterns underlying most literary works.”
  Combines insights from
    Anthropology         Psychology          History       Comparative Religion
v Explores the artist’s humanity by tracing how the individual imagination uses
  myths and symbols common to different cultures and epochs.
v Key concept: “archetype”, based on Carl Jung’s theory of the collective
  unconscious, a set of primal memories common to the human race, existing
  below each person’s conscious mind, which is commonly shared by all individuals;
  according to Jung, archetypes “trigger the collective unconscious”




                                                                      elev8rstraining@gmail.com
Mythological/Archetypal Approach
v Northrop Frye, defined archetypes in a more limited way as “a symbol, usually an
  image, which recurs often enough in literature to be recognizable as an element of
  one’s literary experience as a whole.”
v Frye asserted that literature consists of variations on a great mythic theme that
  contains the following:
   o the creation and life in paradise: garden
   o displacement or banishment from paradise: alienation
   o a time of trial and tribulation, usually a wandering: journey
   o a self-discovery as a result of struggle: epiphany
   o a return to paradise: rebirth/resurrection

Romantic Theory of Criticism
v This impressionistic and individualistic theory emphasizes that literary works must
  be judged based on the impressions they produce and not with reference to rules.
v Launched by W. Wordsworth together with Samuel Taylor Coleridge; strongly
  believed in the power of the human mind to imagine and then create literature
v William Wordsworth explained his idea on romanticism in his Preface to the
  Second Edition of the Lyrical Ballads; he posited that Poetry should
   o have a subject matter that is ordinary and commonplace.
   o use simple language, even aspiring to the language of prose.
   o make use of the imagination.
   o convey a primal (simple, uncomplicated) feeling.
   o present similitude in dissimilitude (similarities in differences).
