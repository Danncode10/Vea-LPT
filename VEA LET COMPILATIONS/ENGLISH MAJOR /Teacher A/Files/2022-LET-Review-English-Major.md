Linguistic Theories and Models
BEHAVIORISM
The behaviorists, most notably BF Skinner, believe that for language learning to occur, imitation, drills, and constant practice are very vital. Behaviorists demand that language teachers must be proficient enough or possess native-like fluency so that what will be taught to the learners are only the perfect ones.
NATIVISM / INNATISM
Noam Chomsky, the main proponent of nativism, criticized the imitation and stimulus-response learning principles of behaviorism for its failure to explain why, with limited knowledge of grammatical rule, people are able to generate limitless number of sentences. With this notion, Chomsky hypothesized that humans must be born with Language Acquisition Device (LAD) in their brain which is the reason why children can acquire language quickly. He argued that LAD contains the universal rules by which human languages operate. He called this set of rule as a Universal Grammar (UG) or mental grammar which allows us to “feel” unconsciously whether or not our utterance or sentence is well-formed.

Proponents of interactionism assert that language acquisition has both biological and social components - - - that is, it is the result the application of the principles propounded by the behaviorists and nativists. Language Competence versus Language Performance
Distinctions of Linguistic Approaches
According to Chomsky, competence is the speaker’s knowledge of the grammatical rules of language while performance is the actual use of language and application of the grammatical rules.
Language competence makes the speakers compose a chain of sentences in different structural patterns and allows them to recognize errors when they are committed as well as vague constructions and fragments. Language Performance is not stable in the sense that it is always dependent on the circumstances of the speaker, which includes social, psychological, and physical factors.

Phonetics deals with the definite production and acoustics of the sounds of language. It looks into the speech production and the interaction of the different organs of speech such as the teeth, the tongue, the lips to articulate a certain sound. It is the study of the physical qualities of speech.
Phonemics looks into the structure of language in terms of phonemes or its distinct units of sounds. A phoneme is the smallest unit of sound in a specific language that differentiates one word from another, as examples, the sounds of /p/, /d/, /b/, /t/ in words pat, dab, pad, bad, and tab.
An allophone is predictable phonetic variations or any speech sounds that stand as a single phoneme, such as the aspirated /k/ in “kit”, and the unaspirated /k/ in “skit”, which are allophones of the phoneme /k/, or the aspirated /t/ in “top” and unaspirated /t/ in “stop” and the flap sound of the /tt/ in <<battery>> are allophones of phoneme /t/.
In English the t sounds in the words “hit,” “tip,” and “little” are allophones; phonemically they are considered to be the same sound although they are different phonetically in terms of aspiration, voicing, and point of articulation.

7 TYPES OF PHONOLOGICAL RULES
1. ASSIMILATION – phonological process in which a sound changes to resemble a nearby sound and can occur both forward and backward.
Ex. The prefix in- where sometimes it appears as in– and others as im-. In front of bilabial words, in– becomes im-. This also happens across word boundaries, like in between pronounced with an m.
2. DISSIMILATION – phonological process in which two close sounds changes to become less alike.
Ex. Manner dissimilation where a stop becomes a fricative when followed by another stop. The word sixth is pronounced sikst, /sθ/ becomes /st/.
3. INSERTION – phonological process in which a sound is added to a word.
Ex. Voiceless stop insertion where between a nasal consonant and a voiceless fricative, a voiceless stop with the same place of articulation as the nasal consonant is inserted. In English, many say hampster instead of hamster, a /p/ is added.

--- Slide 34 ---

4. DELETION – phonological process in which speech sounds disappear from words.
Ex. English is a fast/common speech language, so vowels can be deleted to make the word one syllable, and easier to pronounce in a fast manner. Police becomes plice, and friendship is said as frienship.
5. METATHESIS – phonological process in which sounds switch places in the phonemic structure of a word.
Ex. To make words easier to pronounce and understand, letters are switched. Two historical examples include Old English (brid and aks) becoming Modern English (bird and ask).
6. STRENGTHENING (fortition) – phonological process in which a sound is made stronger.
Ex. Aspiration is where voiceless stops become aspirated when they occur at the beginning of a stressed syllable. Top is said with as h.
7. WEAKENING (lenition) – phonological process in which a sound becomes weaker.
Ex. The definition of flapping is before a stressed vowel and before and unstressed vowel where the sound is pronounced with articulation resembling a flap. The word kitty is an example where the alveolar stop is realized as /r/.

A root is a morph which  the component of a word-form that is intact when all affixes are removed.  Roots are always present in every meaningful linguistic unit. In the form “unstoppable”, the roots is “stop” where the affixes “un and able”, are attached. Significantly, there are two roots in the word “armchair”, the “arm” and the “chair”
A stem is concerned with inflectional morphology. Most words are stems, where affixes can be attached. In the word “unstoppable”, the stem is “stoppable”, which could be broken to a smaller stem “stop”.

Syntactic Categories
1. NOUNS are names. These names may be abstract or concrete. Anything that names a concrete object, person, place, thing, and animal or an abstract idea, feeling, and sentiments is a noun.
2. PRONOUNS are noun substitutes. They are often used in the sentence to avoid mentioning the same noun repeatedly.

3. VERBS are words that denote activities, whether they are action words or words of being. Verbs could be main verbs, be-verbs, do-verbs, have-verbs, modals and participial verbs. Main verbs are action verbs while the rest will either link or function as an auxiliary and act as helping verbs. Be-verbs include is, am, are, was, and were. Do-verbs are do, did, and does. Have-verbs are has, have, and had. Modals include will, would, shall, should, ought to, might, can, could, etc. It is important to take note that be, do, have, and modals serve both the main verb and the auxiliary verb.

Lexical Relations
WORDS are categorized especially in terms of the sounds and structures that make up them up. This way contributes to the characterization of their meaning. However, there is another way of characterizing their meaning – that is by way of identifying its relationship to other words or lexical relations.
1. SYNONYMY (most commonly understood lexical relation)
Synonyms are words with similar or very closely related meanings. Hence, oftentimes, but not always, they are intersubstitutable in sentences. Examples of synonyms include small – tiny, big – huge, fast – quick, wealthy – rich, and many more.
2. ANTONYMY. Antonyms are words with opposite meanings. Commonly used examples are big – small, happy-sad, long-short, rich-poor, hot-cold, true – false. There are two main types of antonyms – gradable or continuous and non-gradable or categorical.

--- Slide 85 ---

3. HYPONYMY. Hyponymy describes a hierarchical relationship between words. If we can say that the meaning of word “x” is embedded in the word “y”, or if x is a kind of y, a hyponymous relationship between x and y exist.
An example is when a pupil is asked about what dolphins are, the pupil answered that dolphins are mammals. In this case, the child identifies the dolphins as mammals.
4. HOMONYMY. ‘Homonyms’ is the broad term used to describe words that are homophones or homographs, or both. Hence, from this definition words such as sea (body of water), see (to watch) and row (argument), row (propel with oars), row (a linear arrangement) are homonyms.
Words are homophones if they are pronounced identically but have different meanings and regardless of spellings. If they have the same spelling then they are also homographs (and homonyms). Examples are sea – see, know – no, meat – meet, cell – sell, male – mail, pale – pail.

5. POLYSEMY. Words that have related meanings and have the same spelling and pronunciation are technically known as polysemy.
Few examples are the words ‘head’ and ‘mouth’ that have multiple meaning which are all related by extension; head – the top part of the body, someone on top of a department or company, and on top of a glass of beer; mouth – part of the human body and that of animals, or the opening of a cave or river.
6. METONYMY. Aside from polysemy, there is another type of lexical relationship that uses a term to stand for another word, idea, or concept. This close association or connection between words is called metonymy.
We can find some examples of metonymy in everyday communication. We use ‘crown’ to refer to the king and his rule, the Malacanang Palace for the President of the Philippines and the federal government, the Wall Street for the U.S economy, and many others.

The Cooperative Principle
Four Maxims (Grice, 1989)
Paul Grice, an English language philosopher, argues that our utterance is a potential interference in the rights, thoughts, and wishes of our listeners.
The Maxim of Quantity. In this maxim, we are reminded that in our talk exchange with people we have to just say enough, nothing more, nothing less. Our contribution must also be informative but just enough, not more informative, as is required of the purpose of the exchange. Hence, we violated this maxim if we said too little, or if we said more than is necessary.
The Maxim of Quality. There is one supermaxim of quality – that is, we have to be truthful. Based on this supermaxim, there are two specific maxims or submaxims:  (1) We should not say what we believe to be false. Since we cannot be sure all the time that what we are going to say is true, the best thing that we should do is to say only what we believe to be true. (2) We should not say anything that we are not sure of or if we lack adequate evidence. We should avoid saying things that we cannot back up with supporting evidence.

The Maxim of Relation. This maxim is also called the maxim of Relevance, following its two-word dictum that we must “Be relevant.” To be relevant means that in a conversation, we should say things related not only to the context of the discourse but also to the context of the situation. In other words, we must not include irrelevant information.
The Maxim of Manner. How we express our thoughts matters in a talk exchange. Often, problems in communication such as misunderstanding or miscommunication occur because of language ambiguity and obscurity of expression. To avoid these problems, we should be perspicuous – that is, we should communicate clearly. We should also use language that is easy to understand. Let us avoid language which can be interpreted in multiple ways. In our communication, we should provide information in a concise manner and orderly fashion. Lastly, let us always “Seek first to understand and to be understood.”

3. Point of View
The point of view of a story determines who is telling it and the narrator's relationship to the characters in the story
First Person Point of View
The narrator is a person in the story, telling the story from their own point of view. The narration usually utilizes the pronoun I (or we, if the narrator is speaking as part of a group). The character who tells the story might be in the middle of the action or more of a character who observes the action from the outer limits, but in either case you are getting that character’s recounting of what happens.
Second Person Point of View
Second-person narration, a little-used technique of narrative in which the action is driven by a character, ascribed to the reader, one known as you. The reader is immersed into the narrative as a character involved in the story. The narrator describes what "you" do and lets you into your own thoughts and background.

--- Slide 138 ---
LET REVIEW – ENGLISH MAJOR
Third Person Point of View
The narrator exists outside the events of the story, and relates the actions of the characters by referring to their names or by the third-person pronouns he, she, or they. Third-person narration can be further classified into several types: omniscient, limited, and objective.
Third Person Omniscient
Omniscient means "all-knowing," and likewise an omniscient narrator knows every character’s thoughts, feelings, and motivations even if that character doesn’t reveal any of those things to the other characters. Little Women by Louisa May Alcott serves as a good example of third-person omniscient narration
B. Third Person Limited
The narrator still exists outside the events of the story, but does not know the motivations or thoughts of all the characters. Rather, one character is the driver of the story, and the reader is given a closer peek into that character’s psyche than the others.
Third-Person Objective
The narrator reports the events that take place without knowing the motivations or thoughts of any of the characters. We know little about what drives them until we hear them speak or observe their actions. The resulting tone is often matter-of-fact, not colored by any opinions or commentary, nor of knowledge of what takes place outside the scene.
